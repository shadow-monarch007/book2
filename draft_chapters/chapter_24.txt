The space lasted for three days.

On Saturday afternoon, Maya walked into the workshop. The weather outside was gray and heavy, a thick autumn fog clinging to the brick walls of the industrial estate. When she pushed the heavy metal door open, the bell above it let out a sharp, lonely chime that seemed to hang in the air like dust.

Elias was at his workbench, running a hand plane over a long piece of oak. The rhythmic, scraping whisper of wood on metal was the only sound in the room, but it felt different today—not the soothing, predictable pace of his usual work, but a rapid, almost frantic motion. He stopped the moment the bell rang, his shoulders tensing instantly before he forcibly made them drop.

"Hey," he said.

His voice carried that familiar, careful neutrality, his face a smooth, unreadable mask that gave away absolutely nothing.

"Hey," Maya replied.

She didn't walk over to the small kitchen corner, and she didn't climb onto her usual stool. She stood by the heavy metal door, her hands shoved deep into the pockets of her dark coat, the weight of the silence between them feeling heavy, rigid, and brittle.

"Are we going to talk about it?" she asked.

Her voice was calm, completely level, but there was a sharp, cold tremor of adrenaline running just underneath the surface.

Elias set the hand plane down on the workbench with a slow, deliberate click. He didn't look at her immediately; he stared down at the long, clean curl of wood shavings on the bench, his jaw locked tight. "Talk about what?"

"The fact that you've been hiding from me since Wednesday morning," Maya said. She didn't raise her voice, didn't accuse him, and didn't yell. She just stated the flat, unvarnished fact of the last three days.

Elias looked up, his dark eyes locked onto hers. The careful, stoic mask cracked, revealing a sudden, raw flash of real frustration. He leaned his hip against the workbench, his arms crossed over his chest, his posture defensive.

"I haven't been hiding, Maya," he said, his voice a low, rough rumble. "I've been working. I have three commissions due by the end of the month."

"Elias."

She said his name with a soft, devastating weight that stopped him in his tracks.

"I know what you look like when you're working," she said, her eyes refusing to let him look away. "And I know what you look like when you're retreating because you're scared. This is the second one. You've been spending twelve hours a day at this bench, sending me texts that look like they were written by a contractor, and slipping into my bed like a ghost. You are hiding."

Elias let out a slow, heavy breath, his hand running through his dark hair, leaving it messy. He stepped forward, the distance between them shrinking by a few feet, his face completely exposed in the harsh light of the work lamp.

"I'm not used to this, Maya," he said, his voice dropping to a rough, quiet confession. "I'm not used to... taking up this much space in someone else's life. I've spent thirty years operating on the assumption that if I ask for more, if I take up too much room, people start looking for the door. I was trying to make sure I wasn't crowding you. I was trying to give you space to breathe."

"You're deciding what I need without asking me!" Maya fired back, her voice finally rising, her hands coming out of her pockets. "You got terrified of how close we were on Wednesday morning. You got scared because I adjusted your collar and it felt too real, too permanent, so you backed off to protect yourself. And you're dressing it up as 'protecting me' so you don't have to admit you're terrified."

The words hit the air between them like stones.

Elias’s jaw locked, his eyes turning dark and fierce. He leaned forward over the workbench, his large hands gripping the edge of the wood so hard his knuckles turned white.

"You're right," he said, his voice a hard, vibrating rasp that filled the room. "I did get scared. But don't stand there and pretend this is all on me, Maya. I got scared because for the last four years, your default response to any kind of pressure has been to run. You spent three years packing yourself into boxes for an ex who didn't even have the decency to say goodbye to your face. You spent years training yourself to believe that the second things get too intense, you have to pull away to protect yourself. I'm trying to give you the space you need so you don't look at me, get terrified of how much I care, and disappear on me."

Maya felt the breath knock clean out of her lungs.

It was incredibly unfair. It was a low blow, dragging up the ghosts of her ex and her own defensive patterns.

It was also, she realized with a sickening, cold jolt in her stomach, completely and utterly true.

She had spent the last week hyper-aware of his walls, but she hadn't looked at her own. She had spent the last three days waiting for him to run, assuming that his distance was the beginning of the end, ready to pack her things and retreat to her own apartment the second he gave the word. She had been braced for the drop, ready to run before she could be left.

"I haven't run from you, Elias," she said, her voice shaking, her eyes burning with sudden, hot tears.

"Not yet," he said.

The flat, heavy resignation in his voice was worse than any anger. He looked down at the workbench, his shoulders dropping, his presence shrinking back into the shadows of the room. "But I've spent my whole life watching the people I step close to walk away when the utility runs out. I'm not going to be the reason you decide this is too heavy and leave."

They stared at each other across the dusty, cold space of the workshop.

Neither of them was wrong. That was the tragedy of it. They were two people whose survival instincts were fundamentally, structurally incompatible: he hid to protect others from his weight, and she ran to protect herself from their absence.

Maya looked down at the concrete floor, fighting the tears that threatened to spill over her eyelashes. Her chest felt hollow, a cold, empty draft blowing through her ribs.

"I can't do this, Elias," she whispered, her voice barely carrying over the hum of the space heater. "I can't be in a relationship where I have to constantly prove I'm not going to leave, just so you won't push me away first."

Elias didn't answer. He didn't look up from the wood shavings.

Maya turned around, pushed the heavy glass door open, and walked out. The bell let out one last, lonely chime, and then the door clicked shut, leaving the workshop empty.

---

Sunday was completely, utterly unbearable.

The silence in her apartment was deafening, a thick, heavy static that made it impossible to focus. Her drawing tablet sat dark on her desk, the blank digital canvas she had opened hours ago remaining untouched. She walked from the kitchen to the living room, her footsteps echoing against the walls, the space feeling too big, too empty, and too cold.

On Monday afternoon, the buzzer next to her door went off.

Her heart skipped a beat, a sudden, violent surge of adrenaline hitting her chest as she ran to the entryway. She pushed the intercom. "Elias?"

"No, it's Liam," his voice came through the scratchy speaker. "I have some mail of yours that got sent to my building."

Maya let out a slow, deflating breath, the disappointment tasting like copper. "Come up."

A minute later, Liam walked through the door. He didn't have his usual, high-energy grin, and he wasn't carrying a joke. He looked at her pale, exhausted face, the dark shadows under her eyes, and the quiet clutter of her apartment. He set a stack of misdelivered envelopes on the kitchen island.

He didn't make a scene. He just walked over, set his hand gently on her shoulder, and gave it a firm, steady squeeze.

"It's going to be okay, Maya," Liam said softly, his voice carrying a rare, serious warmth.

"It doesn't feel okay, Liam," she whispered, her forehead resting against his shoulder as the tears finally began to spill over her cheeks.

Across town, the workshop was dark, with only a single, bright work lamp illuminating the center bench.

Elias was standing blankly in front of a ruined piece of steam-bent wood. He had applied too much heat, too fast, trying to force the curve, and the dark walnut had splintered down the grain, a clean, jagged break that could not be repaired.

The metal door clicked open, and the bell chimed.

Elias didn't look up. "We're closed."

"I don't care," Sam’s voice came from the entryway.

Sam walked through the shop, his boots making a heavy, slow sound on the concrete. He stopped right at the edge of the workbench, looking at the splintered piece of walnut, and then brought his eyes up to meet Elias’s dark, hollow gaze.

Sam didn't offer a platitude, and he didn't ask how he was feeling.

"You're an idiot, Elias," Sam said, his voice flat, deadpan, and completely serious.

Elias’s jaw tensed. "You don't know what happened, Sam."

"I know you're standing in a dark room staring at a broken piece of wood because you were too scared to hold onto the real thing," Sam said. He reached into his pocket, pulled out his car keys, and tossed them onto the workbench. "You've spent thirty years being the foundation for everyone else. But a foundation that doesn't let anyone actually build a home on it is just a hole in the ground."

Sam turned around and walked out, the metal door clicking shut behind him.

Elias stood perfectly still in the dark shop, the silence ringing in his ears.
