Elias had been carrying the small, wooden box in his left jacket pocket for exactly twenty-one days.

He hadn't intended to carry it this long. He had picked up the ring on a cold Tuesday in mid-February, fully intending to ask her on Thursday evening when they had reservations at their favorite dimly lit corner tavern by the harbor.

But Thursday had been a disaster.

The rain had been coming down sideways, the wind rattling the streetlamps on the avenue, and Maya had returned from a five-hour client battle with a brutal, blinding migraine. She had crawled straight into bed, her face pale, the lights in the bedroom turned completely off, the room a dark sanctuary of silence.

Elias had spent the evening sitting on the edge of the bed in the dark, quietly replacing the damp washcloth on her forehead, rubbing her temples with slow, steady circles, and holding her hand until her breathing finally regulated and she fell asleep at 8:00 PM. He had brought her a small glass of water, speaking only in a low, gravelly whisper that was meant to keep the noise of the world out.

He had thought about asking her then, while she was sleeping in the quiet room, just so the weight of the secret would be done. The ring box was in his pocket, warm against his hip, but he had decided against it for obvious logistical and romantic reasons. She deserved better than a proposal delivered to a migraine in the dark. She deserved better than a question asked in silence.

Then he tried the following weekend during a walk through the coastal park.

The sky had been a beautiful, pale blue, the winter sun bright on the snow-covered grass. They had been walking hand-in-hand, their boots crunching on the frozen gravel, the mood perfect, the setting quiet. Elias had reached into his pocket, his fingers brushing the smooth walnut of the box, his heart doing a sudden, violent surge against his ribs.

He had just cleared his throat to start the sentence when a rogue golden retriever, a massive, high-energy animal with zero social boundaries and a muddy tail, had bolted out of the trees.

The dog had bounded straight toward them, leaped up, and playfully snatched Maya's yellow wool scarf clean off her neck before sprinting away down the trail. What followed was a highly chaotic, thirty-minute chase through the muddy woods, with Liam (who happened to be walking nearby, holding a coffee) shouting entirely useless, visually aggressive advice from the sidelines, and Elias trying to maintain his professional dignity while chasing a canine thief through the brush.

"Grab the tail, Elias!" Liam had screamed, nearly spilling his coffee. "He’s using a diagonal trajectory! You need to intercept his vector!"

"I'm not tackling a dog, Liam!" Elias had yelled back, his boots slipping in the mud.

By the time they got the scarf back, the romantic pacing of a proposal had been completely, systematically ruined.

Maya had been laughing too hard to breathe, her boots covered in mud, her face bright with a massive, happy smile, and Elias had simply closed his hand around the box in his pocket, letting out a long, quiet sigh of resignation.

Maya didn't know about the ring.

Everyone else did.

Elias had told Liam first, mostly because he needed someone to hold onto the paper receipt so he wouldn't accidentally leave it on the kitchen island where Maya would find it.

Liam had been barely containable ever since. He practically vibrated with the pressure of the secret, dropping entirely unsubtle, visually aggressive hints about 'metals,' 'forever,' and 'structural commitments' whenever Maya was in the room, which Maya, used to his default high-energy chaos, completely ignored.

Chloe definitely suspected.

She hadn't said a single word about it, an astonishing, near-miraculous display of restraint that Elias knew was costing her physical pain, but every time Elias walked into a room, she would give his left jacket pocket a very pointed, calculating, and triumphant look.

Sam knew because Elias had used the secondary workbench in the workshop to finish the small wooden box.

Elias had spent three late nights sanding the dark walnut wood down to a seamless, silk-like polish, the joints invisible, the small brass hinges aligned to the fraction of a millimeter. Sam had stood nearby, organizing his drill bits, watching him sand in a silent, steady focus. When Elias had finally finished, Sam had walked over, inspected the box, nodded once in deep, professional approval, and handed him a sheet of ultra-fine, thousand-grit sandpaper.

"For the corners," Sam had said. "So it doesn't snag on her pocket."

It was currently week three of The Carrying.

Elias was sitting in their usual booth at the diner with Liam. Liam was glaring at him across a plate of cold fries, his arms crossed over his chest, his face bright red with frustration.

"It's been twenty-one days, Elias," Liam hissed, his voice a harsh, rapid whisper that carried over the diner hum. "I am going to explode. Physically spontaneously combust. My doctor said my blood pressure is too high, and it is entirely because I am carrying the structural weight of your marital future. I can't hold this database anymore."

"I'm waiting for the right moment," Elias said calmly, taking a slow sip of his black coffee.

"You've had six right moments!" Liam yelled, gesturing wildly with a french fry. "You had the anniversary of the day you met on the fire escape. You had the gallery showcase. You had that really good, aesthetically pleasing sunset on Tuesday! The lighting was literally HSL tailored, Elias! It was a perfect gradient!"

"The lighting at the gallery was aggressive," Elias explained, his tone level and deadpan. "And the sunset on Tuesday was interrupted by a fire engine siren. The acoustic interference was too high. It would have ruined the alignment of the sentence."

Liam buried his face in his hands, letting out a muffled groan of absolute despair. "You are over-architecting this, Thorne. You're waiting for some perfect, geometrically flawless alignment of the stars. You want the wind to be blowing at exactly three miles per hour to the southeast, the temperature to be sixty-two degrees, and Maya to be wearing steel-gray."

Elias frowned, setting his mug down. "It should be right, Liam. It’s a permanent decision. The joists should be level. You don't build a roof on a sloped foundation."

Liam dropped his hands.

He looked at Elias across the Formica table. All the usual, high-energy joking and dramatic sighing vanished from his face, replaced by a sudden, startling clarity that Elias had never seen in his younger brother before.

"Elias," Liam said seriously, his voice soft. "There is no right moment. There's no perfect, geometrically flawless alignment of the stars. There’s just the moment you decide to do it."

Elias stared at him.

The words hung in the quiet air between them, matching the low, steady hum of the diner. It was the wisest, most profound thing Liam had ever said. Elias knew, with absolute certainty, that Liam would never let him forget he had said it, but he couldn't deny the truth of it.

He had spent his whole life trying to build the perfect frames, trying to ensure every joint was mathematically flawless before he stepped out of his corner, terrified of the exposure if the structure failed. But their relationship wasn't a blueprint. It was a lived reality, a beautiful, messy, and non-symmetrical home they had built together out of small, ordinary Sundays and burnt pasta sauces.

There was no perfect moment. There was just them.

"You're right," Elias said softly.

Liam’s eyes widened, his dramatic expression returning instantly. He pointed a french fry directly at Elias’s face. "I want that etched onto a brass plaque. I want it on my tombstone: *Liam Thorne, the Architect of His Brother’s Happiness*. Tell Maya I expect a custom dresser as tribute."

During those three weeks of Carrying, Elias had spent his late nights at the workshop talking to Sam. Sam was the only one who didn't tease him, the only one who didn't demand updates on the proposal's timeline. Sam would simply stand by the workbench, sorting his tools, offering a quiet, steady presence that Elias appreciated more than he could say. When Elias had finally finished the wooden box, Sam had walked over, run his hand along the walnut joints, and said, "It's a good piece, Elias. She'll say yes." It was a simple, quiet reassurance from a fellow craftsman, a silent blessing that had given him the courage to keep the box in his pocket.

Elias had brushed the fine maple dust off his canvas coat, looking around the quiet workshop as the winter twilight faded. He smiled to himself, a slow, genuine huff of amusement, thinking of Maya's bright laugh during the golden retriever chase. The memory of her face, covered in mud and completely happy, had chased away the last shadow of his fear. He was ready to ask, ready to build their shared home, and he knew he didn't need the stars to align.

Elias smiled, his hand sliding into his left jacket pocket, his rough fingers brushing the cool, smooth walnut of the small wooden box. He gave it a firm, steady squeeze, the weight of the secret no longer feeling like a burden, but a beautiful, solid promise.
